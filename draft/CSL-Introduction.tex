\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Introduction}



Cyclic proof systems are the formalization of a particular way of reasoning with inductively defined predicates, where derivations using induction rules are replaced by infinite regular proof trees, whose infinite branches satisfy the ``infinite descent'' condition. 
Following Fermat's method, if such a branch exists in a proof using the definition of inductive predicates infinitely many times, then an infinite sequence of decreasing-size values of inductively defined formal parameters should exist, contradicting their well-founded definition. As a consequence, a cyclic proof encodes an underlying inductive argument that is sound.

The infinite descent, also called {\em global trace condition} (GTC) in the literature, is the adaptation of Fermat's 
principle to cyclic proofs in sequent calculi, extended with Martin-Löf's  inductive definitions \cite{MartinLof}, as adopted by Brotherston and Simpson
\cite{Brotherston1, Brotherston2,BrotherstonSimpson}, where it implies the correctness of the 
conclusion of an infinite proof-tree \cite[Proposition 4.2.10]{Brotherston1}, or equivalently of a certain finite directed graph with cycles, hence the name. 
Counterintuitive as it may be, a good strategy 
to look for a finite proof is to look for a regular and \emph{infinite} proof satisfying GTC first.
Indeed, the use of GTC combines well both with proof search and with program synthesis
on realistic examples \cite{10.1007/978-3-642-35182-2_25,10.1145/3632888, 10.1007/978-3-031-90643-5_18,  10.1145/3453483.3454087,  10.1145/3018610.3018623, Stratulat_2021, Brotherston2020}. 

As observed in \cite{Brotherston1}, GTC is a termination argument.

On the other hand, in \cite{Lee1} Lee, Jones, and Ben-Amram have
introduced the \emph{size-change termination} condition (SCT) 
to provide a sufficient condition for the totality of partial recursive maps on natural numbers,
initiating a thread of research on the termination of functional programs, see \cite{Lee2,Lee3} and references there.
A major result in \cite{Lee1} is that proving that SCT holds for a set of graphs, called size-change graphs, is a PSPACE-complete problem.

In her recent study \cite{Mirai}, Ikebuchi has shown a deep relationship among GTC and SCT, by proving that a set of size-change graphs can be associated with each cyclic pre-proof so that it satisfies GTC if and only if the graphs associated with it satisfy SCT.

In this paper, we go further in investigating this relation between cyclic proofs and SCT. 
In \cite{Lee2,Lee3}, Lee and Ben-Amran have explored the complexity of the problem of deciding the SCT condition by
considering some particular cases of size-change graphs. For what is of concern here, in the case of fan-in free graphs, namely when the in-degree of all nodes is at most one, the SCT problem can be solved within exponential time, in place of the double or triple exponential needed in the general case. By considering a sequent calculus in the original sense of Gentzen \cite{}, where sequents are pairs of (finite) lists instead of multisets or sets of formulas as in Brotherston and Simpson systems, we show that the notion of trace in a pre-proof can be defined via a fan-in free size-change graph.

C.S. Lee and A. Ben-Amram considered , including
SCT for fan-in-free graphs, namely, for graphs in which no nodes has two incoming edges
(\cite{Lee2} and \cite{Lee3}, Section 2.4. \quotationMarks{Subclasses of SCT}). 
We want to study cyclic proofs associated to fan-in-free graphs. 

There are several advantages
in considering this restricted class. GTC can be expressed an essentially weaker condition
in the case of fan-in free graphs, and it can be checked faster. 
In a future work, we hope to show that we can show the correctness of cyclic proofs 
satisfying GTC can be obtained by a simpler proof in the case of fan-in free graphs.

The original version of cyclic proofs by Brotherston and Simpson, named $\CLKIDomega$, 
is \emph{not} fan-in free. As a counterexample, consider the following instance of left-equality 
rule, using sequents as sets, where $N\mathbf{t}$ means: 
\quotationMarks{$\mathbf{t}$ is an inductively defined natural number}.

 \begin{center}
    %EQUALITY LEFT
    %-----------------------------------------
    \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{ \Gamma, \vnode1{N\mathbf{t}} \vdash \Delta}
        \infer1[(=L)]{\Gamma, t = u, \vnode2{N\mathbf{t}}, \vnode3{N\mathbf{u}} \vdash \Delta} 
    \end{prooftree}

    % DRAW EDGES
    $
    \dirflowblueedges{node3/node1, node2/node1} 
    $
  \end{center}
  
If we apply Brotherston's definition for trace (details can be found in 
\cite{Brotherston1}, Definition 4.2.3), we obtain that the inductive formula $N\mathbf{t}$ 
in the premise has at least two incoming edges obtained from the trace relation, from the inductive formulas $N\mathbf{t}$ and $N\mathbf{u}$
in the conclusion. Even considering sequents as multisets, fan-in free connections
between inductive formulas are not guaranteed. For example, consider the left-weakening rule:
    
    \begin{center}
      \begin{prooftree}
        \hypo{\Gamma, \vnode1{N\mathbf{u}} \vdash \Delta}
        \infer1[(WeakL)]{\Gamma, \vnode2{N\mathbf{u}}, \vnode3{N\mathbf{u}} \vdash \Delta} 
      \end{prooftree}

      % DRAW EDGES
    $
    \dirflowblueedges{node3/node1, node2/node1} 
    $  
    \end{center}

where one of the inductive predicates $N\mathbf{u}$ in the conclusion is a direct descendant of the inductive predicate in the premise, while the other was freshly introduced by the weakening rule.

In this paper we introduce a variant of Brotherston's cyclic proofs in which connections
between atomic inductive formulas, given by the trace relation, are fan-in free. To this aim, we represent left and right
hand side of a sequent with a list of formulas, and trace \emph{occurrences} instead of formulas. We will check that the set of theorem we can derive does not change, while the algorithm
checking GTC has a simpler formulation in this case.
